Hungry Gaze

Visual Meaning-Making Practices in a YouTube Channel

By Youngjae  ·  EST 240 Visual Rhetoric & Information Technology  ·  SUNY Korea
Instructed by Dr. Anthony J. Pennings

The primary video analyzed throughout this essay: The Legendary Floating Noodles. PAIK JONG WON, 2024.

Introduction

The rise of YouTube as a global media platform has fundamentally transformed how food is seen, consumed, and understood. Among the many genres that have emerged, food travel mukbang — in which a host eats local food abroad while reacting on camera — has gained enormous international viewership. The YouTube channel PAIK JONG WON, operated by South Korean food entrepreneur and television personality Paik Jong-won, exemplifies this genre. With over 6.27 million subscribers and more than 900 uploaded videos, the channel's bilingual name — combining the Korean "백종원" with the romanized "PAIK JONG WON" — signals its dual address to both domestic and international audiences. At the heart of the channel is the Baegopa ("I'm Hungry") series, in which Paik travels to cities including Hong Kong, Bangkok, Macau, and Istanbul to explore local food culture. Notably, the channel operates without advertising revenue, yet invests in ten or more cameras per shoot and maintains a full editing and sound team — a production scale that rivals broadcast television, and one that makes clear that every visual choice is deliberate.

This paper focuses on two episodes from the Baegopa series: the first, in which Paik eats hand-pulled noodles served from a floating boat in Hong Kong Harbor, and the second, in which he discovers a Turkish street snack in Istanbul that visually resembles a beloved Korean confection called the "Home Run Ball." Both videos were selected because they represent distinct visual environments — one defined by water and physical instability, the other by a busy urban market — while sharing the same underlying structure of visual meaning-making. This paper analyzes the channel through three overlapping frameworks. The first is visual grammar — the systematic use of shot size, camera angle, movement, editing, and sound as described by Chandler (2021), including the concept of mise-en-scène as the total arrangement of meaning within a single frame. The second is semiotics, drawing on Barthes's three orders of signification — denotation, connotation, and myth — and on Fiske's application of those orders to moving image media. The third is the structure of the look and desire, drawing on Mulvey's theory of scopophilia and on Pennings's argument that looking is itself a signifying practice. Together these frameworks demonstrate that PAIK JONG WON does not simply document food culture but actively constructs it — engineering trust, generating desire, and naturalizing a specific ideological worldview centered on Korean cultural authority and the myth of authentic food discovery.

The Host and the Frame

Shot Size

The most consistent visual pattern across both videos is a recurring three-part shot size sequence: an establishing shot that sets the spatial context, a close-up that isolates the food, and a medium close-up that captures Paik's emotional reaction. In the first video, the sequence opens with a wide establishing shot of Hong Kong Harbor — the water, the small wooden boat, and the elderly vendor visible in the frame before any food appears. This is followed by extreme close-ups of the noodles being pulled and assembled, the steam rising, and the broth's texture filling the screen. The sequence closes on Paik's face in medium close-up as he takes his first bite, his eyes widening in apparent disbelief. In the second video, the same structure is applied to the Istanbul street stall: an establishing shot of the market exterior, a big close-up of the round golden köfte ball that visually mirrors Korea's "Home Run Ball" snack, and a medium close-up of Paik's exaggerated surprised expression accompanied by the exclamation, "이게 원조라고?" ("This is the original?"). As Chandler notes, close-up shots function to emphasize emotion and establish intimacy, while big close-ups remove contextual information and reduce the subject to its most essential sensory qualities. In both videos, the food close-up operates as an independent sign: stripped of its cultural context, it becomes an image of pure appetite.

Camera Angle and Mise-en-Scène

Both videos rely predominantly on eye-level camera angles when filming Paik himself. Chandler identifies eye-level as the "neutral" angle associated with documentary realism and an absence of power hierarchy — it positions the subject as an equal to the viewer rather than as superior or inferior. This is a deliberate construction of Paik's persona: he is presented not as a distant authority figure but as a friendly, accessible food companion. In the first video, the eye-level is maintained even on the unstable surface of the floating boat, reinforcing an impression of authenticity and spontaneity. In the second video, however, a subtly low angle is used when filming the köfte ball resting on the vendor's counter. By looking slightly upward at the food, the camera elevates the object, treating it as a subject of reverence. Together, these choices in shot angle, framing, and spatial composition constitute what Chandler identifies as the mise-en-scène of each video — the totality of what is arranged and visible within the frame, prior to any cut. Every element of the mise-en-scène in both videos is coordinated toward the same rhetorical end: to construct Paik as trustworthy because he is like us, and the food as worthy because it is above us. Stuart Hall (1997) argued that visual media require a figure who anchors meaning and prevents the audience from drifting toward unwanted interpretations. Paik's eye-level presence — calm, unhurried, dressed without visible branding — performs exactly that anchoring function.

Camera Movement

The two videos employ contrasting but equally meaningful camera movements. In the first video, the hand-held camera is unavoidable aboard the floating boat — the vessel's natural motion produces a gentle, constant instability in the image. Chandler associates hand-held footage with urgency, immediacy, and chaos; here, it connotes documentary authenticity and the physical reality of being present on the water. The viewer is not watching from a comfortable distance but is placed, visually, on the rocking boat. In the second video, the camera uses a tracking shot as Paik walks through the Istanbul market street, physically moving alongside him through the crowd. Chandler distinguishes tracking from zooming: a zoom changes the focal length of the lens while the camera remains stationary, producing a different visual quality than the full-body movement of a tracking shot. The tracking movement creates a sense of shared journey — the viewer accompanies Paik through the market rather than simply observing him from outside. Across both videos, the alternation between hand-held instability and smooth tracking produces a visual rhythm that shifts between raw immersion and narrative guidance, reinforcing the ethos of a host who is both present in the moment and in control of the story.

The Grammar of Appetite

Editing

The editing structure in both videos follows what Chandler calls "motivated cutting" — transitions that occur precisely when the viewer's curiosity is aroused, making cuts feel natural and inevitable rather than artificial. In the first video, the editing cross-cuts between the noodle master preparing the dish on the rocking boat and Paik waiting, watching, and reacting. This parallel editing — showing two simultaneous actions in different spaces — builds narrative tension and gives the food a sense of journey before it reaches the bowl. In the second video, jump cuts are employed during Paik's verbal explanation of the köfte ball's resemblance to the Korean snack, paired with rapid Korean-language subtitles. The resulting rhythm is fast, playful, and energetic — matching Paik's excited tone and catering to the shortened attention spans characteristic of YouTube viewership. Both videos also demonstrate what Chandler identifies as "invisible editing" — the Hollywood convention of concealing the mechanics of editing so that the viewer experiences a seamless narrative flow rather than a constructed sequence of shots. Dramatically, both videos follow the same arc identified by Pennings as the classic structure of visual narrative: a slow buildup of anticipation, a climactic first bite, and a resolution in the form of Paik's verdict. The editing does not merely record this arc — it manufactures it, engineering the viewer's emotional response at every transition.

Sound Design

Sound in both videos is dominated by diegetic audio — sound that originates within the world on screen. Chandler distinguishes diegetic sound, which includes ambient noise, voices, and environmental sounds present within the film's space, from non-diegetic sound such as background music added in post-production. In the first video, the layered soundscape of the harbor — water lapping against the hull, the creak of the wooden boat, the sound of the broth being poured — anchors the viewer in a physically convincing environment. Non-diegetic music is present but minimal, and never overpowers the ambient sound. In the second video, the sonic texture of the Istanbul market — vendors calling out, traffic in the distance, the sizzle of the köfte on the griddle — performs the same function: it constructs the scene as unmediated and authentic. Both videos also employ selective sound amplification, heightening the sounds of chewing, crunch, and slurp beyond their natural volume. This technique transforms the act of eating into a heightened sensory event for the viewer, producing the kind of visceral, embodied response that Pennings associates with the affective dimension of visual media — a pathos appeal conducted not through argument but through sound. Throughout both videos, Paik's voiceover bridges cuts — a sound bridge that maintains narrative continuity across visual transitions and reinforces his role as the authoritative guide to the experience.

From Image to Ideology

Denotation

The first order of signification, as described by Roland Barthes and applied to visual media by John Fiske, is denotation: the literal, descriptive meaning of an image, free from cultural interpretation. Fiske states plainly that "denotation is what is photographed." At the denotative level, the first video shows a middle-aged Korean man sitting on a small wooden boat in a harbor. A vendor prepares noodles in a bowl beside him. The man eats from the bowl; steam rises from the broth. At the denotative level, the second video shows a middle-aged Korean man standing at an outdoor street stall in a city. He holds a small round fried food item, examines it, and eats it while speaking to the camera. Both descriptions are flat, neutral, and devoid of cultural weight. They describe only what the camera has recorded, in the terms Fiske assigns to denotation: the raw content of the shot. The significance of articulating this denotative baseline is precisely to expose how much meaning is added at the levels of connotation and myth — and how little of the channel's actual communicative power operates at the level of literal fact.

The second video analyzed: The Turkish "Home Run Ball." PAIK JONG WON, 2024.

Connotation

Moving to Barthes's second order of signification, connotation describes the cultural, emotional, and ideological meanings that accumulate around a sign through its use in a specific social context. Fiske writes that "connotation is how it is photographed" — meaning that the visual choices analyzed in the preceding sections are themselves the mechanisms through which connotation is produced. In the first video, the floating boat does not merely denote a mode of delivery; it connotes rarity, inaccessibility, and the romance of tradition preserved outside of modernity. The word "legendary" in the video's title pre-frames the food as mythic before a single image appears, directing the viewer to read what follows through the lens of mythic status. Paik's wide-eyed reaction functions as an indexical sign in Peirce's classification — a response that is causally and directly connected to the quality of the food, and therefore offers visual proof of the food's excellence. In the second video, the connection to the Korean "Home Run Ball" snack operates as a powerful connotative device: the Turkish köfte ball becomes meaningful to Korean viewers not on its own cultural terms but through its resemblance to a familiar domestic sign. Paik's exclamation "이게 원조라고?" encodes a connotation of cultural discovery — but one framed entirely by Korean referential knowledge.

Myth

Barthes's third order of signification — myth — describes the process by which historically and culturally constructed meanings are made to appear natural, inevitable, and universal. Barthes writes that myth "transforms history into nature." Three distinct myths operate across the two videos analyzed here. The first is the myth of the expert's gaze as truth: Paik's judgment that food is delicious is received not as one man's opinion but as objective culinary fact. This authority has been so thoroughly naturalized through his decades of media presence that the channel does not need to argue for it — it simply assumes it, and viewers accept it. The second myth, most clearly visible in the second video, is the naturalization of Korean taste as a universal standard: by measuring Turkish food against a Korean snack, the video positions Korean cultural knowledge as the reference point from which the world is evaluated. The phrase "한국인이면 100% 환장할" presents Korean preference not as culturally specific but as a reliable index of quality for any viewer. The third myth, evident in the first video, is the myth that authentic food requires discovery — that truly good food is hidden, rare, difficult to access, and available only to those with an expert guide. This positions Paik, and the channel itself, as essential intermediaries between the viewer and genuine culinary experience. As Barthes demonstrates through his analysis of the Paris Match magazine cover, myth is most effective when it does not announce itself — when it speaks, as he puts it, as nature rather than history.

The Look, Identification, and Desire

The final analytical framework applied here draws on Laura Mulvey's concept of the look and its development by Pennings. Pennings states that "looking is a signifying practice — it is active and interpretative, denotative and connotative." The camera's look in both videos does not passively record; it actively positions the viewer within a structure of identification and desire. In the first video, the camera's placement on the boat and its hand-held movement simulate the physical experience of being present with Paik — the viewer is not watching from outside but placed, through the look of the camera, within the scene. In the second video, the camera lingers on Paik's face as he examines the köfte ball before eating, inviting the viewer to read his anticipation. This constitutes what Mulvey identifies as the look producing desire: the viewer watches Paik desire the food, and through identification with his look, begins to desire it too. Mulvey's concept of scopophilia — the pleasure derived from the act of looking itself — is directly applicable here, though displaced from the human body onto food: the close-up of the noodles, the steam, the bite mark in the köfte ball are all objects of the visual gaze, generating pleasure through the act of being seen. As Pennings notes, "desire is produced from the gaze of others." In the Baegopa series, Paik's visible desire is the engine that drives the viewer's own.

Conclusion

This paper has demonstrated that the YouTube channel PAIK JONG WON constructs visual meaning across three interconnected layers. At the level of visual grammar, consistent choices in shot size, camera angle, movement, editing, and sound design — organized within a carefully controlled mise-en-scène — create a viewing experience that feels simultaneously intimate and authoritative, spontaneous and carefully structured. At the semiotic level, the channel moves from denotation — the literal recording of a man eating abroad — through connotation — cultural meanings built through visual style — to myth, where those meanings are naturalized as universal truths about food, taste, and expertise. At the level of the look, the camera positions the viewer to identify with Paik's experience and to desire what he desires, transforming passive consumption of video content into active appetite. Both videos analyzed here, despite their different settings and foods, reveal the same underlying visual system — confirming that these are not incidental choices but the repeating grammar of a carefully produced channel.

Pennings reminds us that "media do not simply reflect or mirror reality but create new realities in the process of representation." The Baegopa series does not document what food in Hong Kong or Istanbul "really is." It produces a version of those food cultures that is filtered through the look of a Korean expert, structured by the conventions of mukbang and food travel, and shaped by the ideological myths of authenticity, discovery, and cultural authority. Visual literacy — the ability to read these constructions critically — is therefore not merely an academic exercise but a practical necessity. As Barthes argues, myth is most powerful when it goes unnoticed. It is the task of visual analysis, and of the discipline of visual rhetoric, to make it visible.
